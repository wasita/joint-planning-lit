joint planning &
coordination

Where computational cognitive science, behavioral game theory, and multi-agent AI meet on the problem of two or more agents forming and acting on a shared plan. Every entry carries a tier, a note on why it earns its place, and — where relevant — a flag for a common citation trap.

how to read the tiers

Tier 1 (23 papers) — read in full. Tier 2 — skim the introduction and discussion. Tier 3 — know it exists, cite when relevant. Tiers reflect value to someone building computational models of coordination, not the importance of the work in its own field; several Tier 3 entries are classics that simply are not the fastest route in.

The threads are not mutually exclusive and the boundaries are arguable — thread G in particular cuts across A and D on the axis of continuous versus discrete time, which turns out to matter more than the disciplinary split.

01

a four-week path

Sixteen of the papers below, sequenced as roughly four hours a week for a month. The social and joint-planning material comes first deliberately: the single-agent resource-rational work is easier to absorb later, whereas these shape which questions look worth asking.

01foundations: planning, theory of mind, joint intention4 papers · ~4.5 h

Ho, Saxe & Cushman (2022). Planning with theory of mind. Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 26(11), 959–971.

Read this before anything technical. Ho's own framing of the multi-agent planning problem, and a serviceable MDP refresher — it reintroduces the planning formalism in 12 pages of prose rather than 200 of Sutton & Barto. Highest hours-saved-per-hour-spent item on the whole list.

Tier 11.5 hPDF

Ho, Abel, Correa, Littman, Cohen & Griffiths (2022). People construct simplified mental representations to plan. Nature, 606, 129–136.

The flagship. Figures-first is fine — the construct (value-guided construal: people build a simplified task representation before planning over it) matters more here than the methods. The open question worth turning over: what's the joint analogue of a construal? Do two people converge on a shared simplification, and does coordination succeed or fail on that convergence?

Tier 10.75 harXiv

Kleiman-Weiner, Ho, Austerweil, Littman & Tenenbaum (2016). Coordinate to cooperate or compete: abstract goals and joint intentions in social interaction. Proc. 38th CogSci.

The cleanest statement of the hypothesis space this literature lives in: recursive best-response (each agent models the other modeling it) vs. joint intention / we-mode (agents adopt a shared abstract goal and plan from it). Nearly every model in the area is a variant of, or a hybrid between, these two.

Tier 11 hPDF

Ho, MacGlashan, Greenwald, Littman, Hilliard, Trimbach, Brawner, Tenenbaum, Kleiman-Weiner & Austerweil (2016). Feature-based joint planning and norm learning in collaborative games. Proc. 38th CogSci, 1158–1163.

The closest antecedent to the joint-planning program this list circles — Ho's own two-player gridworld, joint planning model, best-response comparison, two experiments. Play the demo and read the repo, not just the paper; it shows what a task in this tradition looks like at the level of pixels and code.

02the behavioral-economics half4 papers · ~4 h

Crawford, Costa-Gomes & Iriberri (2013). Structural models of nonequilibrium strategic thinking: theory, evidence, and applications. Journal of Economic Literature, 51(1), 5–62.

If only one item in this entire guide gets read, make it this. It converts “behavioral game theory” into “a model class with a writable likelihood” — identification, level-0 specification, structural estimation — which is the register Bayesian cognitive modelers already speak. It teaches the game theory as it goes. Read §1–4 and the coordination application; skip auctions and bilateral trade.

Tier 12 hPDF

Colman & Gold (2018). Team reasoning: solving the puzzle of coordination. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 25(5), 1770–1783.

The entry point to team reasoning — written for psychologists, no game theory assumed. The economics-side cousin of the “joint intention” branch. Read it as: the same idea, thirty years earlier, in different notation. Noticing exactly these convergences is much of the payoff of reading across these traditions. (Skip Bacharach's book; read Gold & Sugden's intro to it for more.)

Tier 11 h

Misyak, Melkonyan, Zeitoun & Chater (2014). Unwritten rules: virtual bargaining underpins social interaction, culture, and society. TiCS, 18(10), 512–519.

The third live theory: people coordinate by simulating a negotiation they never actually have, then acting on its imagined outcome. Shortest, clearest statement. It matters disproportionately because it has the most active current modeling program attached (see Week 3).

Tier 10.5 hPDF

Camerer, Ho & Chong (2004). A cognitive hierarchy model of games. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 119(3), 861–898.

Skim for the model, not the applications — the Poisson-CH generative story is the payload, and it is typically the first baseline implemented in simulations of this kind. Pair it with the level-0 problem: what a level-0 agent does is a modeling choice that silently determines everything above it, and it's the most common way these models get quietly mis-specified.

Tier 20.5 hPDF
03models that actually run5 papers · ~5.5 h

Wu, Wang, Evans, Tenenbaum, Parkes & Kleiman-Weiner (2021). Too many cooks: Bayesian inference for coordinating multi-agent collaboration. Topics in Cognitive Science, 13(2), 414–432.

The algorithm to Kleiman-Weiner 2016's hypothesis: Bayesian delegation — agents hold a posterior over sub-task assignments and infer from a partner's behavior who is doing what. Plus human data. The closest thing to a template for both the model and the write-up in this genre.

Tier 11.5 hPDFcite the TopiCS version — arXiv / CogSci / AAMAS variants differ in title and author order

Tang, Gong, Zhao, Zhou, Shen & Gao (2025). Joint commitment in human cooperative hunting through an “imagined we.” Proceedings of the Royal Society B, 292(2055), 20243070.

The Imagined We model — each player infers the intention of a collective “we” agent rather than maximizing individual reward from a shared outcome. Beats reward-sharing models, and the gap widens as the number of possible targets grows. Currently the front-runner in this line of stag-hunt modeling. Cite this Proc B version, not the CogSci 2020/2022 precursors.

Tier 11 hPDFproject

Le Pargneux, Zeitoun, Konstantinidis & Chater (2024). Coordination in dynamic interactions by converging on tacitly agreed joint plans. Proc. 46th CogSci.

Read early and carefully: the nearest published work to the problem this list circles. A virtual-bargaining model of joint plans in dynamic coordination, model-compared against alternatives, two experiments. Knowing precisely what it already covers is how a new project in this space avoids rediscovering it.

Tier 11 hPDF

Kuang, Brockbank, Bicchieri & Hawkins (2026). Inferring joint commitment from observed coordination. Proc. 48th CogSci.

Hawkins again. Two experiments, N = 785, gridworld farm with an explicit stag hunt payoff structure (solo harvest of the center tree yields 1 berry, joint harvest 8). The model is Bayesian ToM over a latent commitment parameter θ ∈ [0,1] that interpolates between individual and collective expected utility — turning we-mode-vs-I-mode from a discrete model comparison into a fitted continuous dial. That single move is the most useful idea on this whole list.

Tier 11 hPDFnot dyadic — third-party observers rating pre-generated stimuli, N = 785 individualsfor commitment ratings the null model slightly beat the full model; only agreement ratings fit well (r = .90)

Baker, Jara-Ettinger, Saxe & Tenenbaum (2017). Rational quantitative attribution of beliefs, desires and percepts in human mentalizing. Nature Human Behaviour, 1, 0064.

Canonical Bayesian theory of mind — familiar inference machinery pointed at a different generative model. This is the one to drop if the week runs long: Kuang et al. above applies BToM directly to the construct at issue, which matters more than the canonical demonstration. For the cheap version instead, see Jara-Ettinger et al. (2016), The naïve utility calculus, TiCS 20(8) — same vocabulary, half the math.

Tier 21 hjournal
04formalism, one hard constraint, one paradigm3 papers · ~4.5 h

Albrecht, Christianos & Schäfer (2024). Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning: Foundations and Modern Approaches. MIT Press. — Ch. 3 and 4 only.

Free, CC-licensed, solves two problems at once. Ch. 3 walks the exact ladder — normal-form → repeated → stochastic (Markov) games → POSGs. Ch. 4 is a compact game-theory bootstrap (best response, Nash, correlated equilibrium, Pareto optimality) without a semester of micro. Read it as a notation dictionary, not a textbook. Skip Ch. 5+; nothing in the joint-planning program here needs deep RL.

Tier 12 hfree book

Thomas, DeScioli, Haque & Pinker (2014). The psychology of coordination and common knowledge. JPSP, 107(4), 657–676.

One paper, one finding, and it constrains the model class before a line of it is written: people treat common knowledge as a categorical state, not a point on a probability continuum. That rules out an entire family of graded-higher-order-belief models that would otherwise be tempting to write down. Cheap insurance.

Tier 11 hPDF

Wu, Deffner, Kahl, Meder, Ho & Kurvers (2025). Adaptive mechanisms of social and asocial learning in immersive collective foraging. Nature Communications, 16, 3539.

Ho is a co-author, and this is the best methodological template available for building a real-time task. It solves the three hard problems: how to define a discrete decision event inside continuous movement, how to reconstruct what each agent could actually see of the other (automated visual-field transcription — essential for conditioning a model on observability), and how to fit a time-varying-weight choice model to trajectory data. Public data on Zenodo. Read the methods, not just the results.

B · virtual bargaining & the current synthesis frontier6 items

A runnable probabilistic virtual-bargaining model. Note the phrase “resource-rational” — Ho's framework colliding with Chater's, which is more or less the intersection this list is organized around.

Bargaining-theoretic constructs (outside options, disagreement points) predicting human judgments about joint action.

C · computational theory of mind & inverse planning7 items

Wang, Davis & Jara-Ettinger (2025). Modeling other minds: a computational account of social cognition and its development. Annual Review of Developmental Psychology, 7.

One of the most important bridges on this page. Its central construct — “restricted scope models,” context-specific simplifications that make social reasoning tractable — is the direct social-cognition analogue of Ho's simplified task representations. Inverse planning where the inferred quantity is the partner's attentional and representational limits. One of the most recent entries in this line, and among the nearest published work to the problems this list circles. Worth reading early.

Tier 32020, not 2021 — commonly miscited

Gilbert (1999) on joint commitment and plural subjects · Searle (1990) on collective intentions · Bacharach (2006).

The philosophical backdrop Kuang et al. builds on. Worth knowing the positions; reading them in full is not urgent. Operationalizes “hierarchical plan” as program structure — the natural formal substrate on which a joint hierarchical plan would be built. “Program-structured plans × joint intentions” is a defensible and currently open direction at this intersection.

Deliberately down-tiered. Everything essential is compressed into the opening and restated in the papers above. Not worth five hours plus commentaries.

Tier 3
F · multi-agent AI & cooperative AI8 items

Mon-Williams, Taylor-Davies, Mieczkowski, Vélez, Bramley, Wang, Griffiths & Lucas (2025). Partner modelling emerges in recurrent agents (but only when it matters). NeurIPS 2025.

Recurrent RL agents in Overcooked develop internal partner models — only when task structure makes partner-modeling instrumentally necessary. Read as an adversarial check on the obvious claims: an argument that human stag-hunt behavior requires recursive partner modeling has to clear what a non-recursive baseline already achieves. The null hypothesis, pre-built.

Tier 1arXiv

Mieczkowski, Mon-Williams, Bramley, Lucas, Vélez & Griffiths (2025). Predicting multi-agent specialization via task parallelizability. arXiv:2503.15703.

An Amdahl's-Law account of when multi-agent systems should specialize vs. generalize, validated across SMAC, MPE and Overcooked. Note the boundary case: a stag hunt has parallelizability ≈ 0, since the whole point is that the stag requires simultaneous joint action. Useful as a theoretical foil rather than a direct tool — and it's a clean example of a simulation-experiment paper.

Tier 2arXiv

Jha, Carvalho, Liang, Du, Kleiman-Weiner & Jaques (2025). Cross-environment cooperation enables zero-shot multi-agent coordination. ICML 2025 (Oral).

The field's current answer: training across many environments, not clever equilibrium selection, is what produces partners humans can work with.

Tier 2

Dafoe, Bachrach, Hadfield, Horvitz, Larson & Graepel (2021). Cooperative AI: machines must learn to find common ground. Nature, 593, 33–36.

Four pages of framing. Read it for the vocabulary the AI side of this audience uses.

Tier 2

Hu, Lerer, Peysakhovich & Foerster (2020). “Other-Play” for zero-shot coordination. ICML 2020.

The best computational formalization of Schelling salience — symmetry-breaking and label-invariance. A rare case where an AI paper is the clearest statement of a cognitive-science idea.

Tier 2PDF

Bard et al. (2020). The Hanabi challenge: a new frontier for AI research. Artificial Intelligence, 280, 103216 — §1–3 only.

The canonical theory-of-mind-under-partial-observability benchmark.

Tier 2

Carroll, Shah, Ho, Griffiths, Seshia, Abbeel & Dragan (2019). On the utility of learning about humans for human-AI coordination. NeurIPS 32.

Ho is third author; introduces Overcooked-AI. The finding that matters: agents trained in self-play coordinate beautifully with copies of themselves and catastrophically with humans. It is the reason cross-play evaluation exists.

Tier 2PDF

Oliehoek & Amato (2016). A Concise Introduction to Decentralized POMDPs. Springer — Ch. 1–2.

Redundant for anyone doing the MARL book in Week 4. Keep as reference for Dec-POMDP specifics. “Concise” is relative.

Tier 3PDF
G · continuous-time & real-time spatial coordination15 items

Nearly everything in threads A–F is discrete and turn-based: one simultaneous move, or a step through a gridworld. This thread is continuous-time and spatial — agents move freely, watch each other move, and commitment emerges over a trajectory rather than in a single choice. The distinction is not cosmetic. It changes which equilibrium gets selected, which models are even expressible, and what a task has to log in order to measure anything.

Deng, … Garg & Mobbs (2026). Linking compromise and responsibility attribution to risky decision-making in dyadic foraging. PNAS. doi:10.1073/pnas.2602570123

Arguably the closest paradigm match to a spatial stag hunt anywhere: 120 solo trials calibrating individual risk tolerance under predation, then 60 rounds of dyadic play where the avatar moves to the midpoint between both players' choices — a literal continuous compromise mechanism — plus post-hoc credit/blame attribution. Self-serving responsibility asymmetry predicts reduced willingness to compromise.

Tier 1partially verified — PNAS blocked direct fetch; confirm the full author list from the DOI before citing

Hawkins & Goldstone (2016). The formation of social conventions in real-time environments. PLOS ONE, 11(3), e0151670.

The one new item to read in Week 4 if nothing else. Real-time interaction produces more efficient and fairer conventions than simultaneous decisions, and high stakes amplify the effect. The methodological point is the one to steal: fine-grained movement trajectories reveal a continuous negotiation process that is completely invisible in discrete-choice data. An argument for continuous time as a measurement instrument, not a design flourish — and it's by a Ho co-author.

Tier 1open access

Hallers-Haalboom, De Jaegher, Sterck, Brosnan & Vermande (2025). Communication increases cooperation among students in a coordination game. PNAS, 122(35), e2517669122.

127 dyads, 40 rounds of online stag hunt, payoffs not disclosed. Communication drives both information discovery and coordination. A useful precedent for online dyadic stag hunt at scale.

Tier 2

Tsutsui, Shinya, Kudo, Müller & Lehmann (2024). Collaborative hunting in artificial agents with deep reinforcement learning. eLife, 13, e85694.

Role differentiation (chaser, blocker, ambusher) emerges from plain multi-agent deep RL with no theory-of-mind machinery. The deflationary alternative that has to be ruled out — cheap sophistication that looks like joint commitment.

Tier 2

Möller, Unakafov, Fischer, Gail, Treue & Kagan (2023). Human and macaque pairs employ different coordination strategies in a transparent decision game. eLife, 12, e80943.

Transparency — mutual real-time observation of the partner's action — is what enables human coordination, and macaques largely don't exploit it. Directly relevant to any task that manipulates what partners see of each other, which most such tasks probably should.

Tier 2

Zhao, Tang, Dahmani, Perry, Zhu, Rossano & Gao (2021). Sharing is not needed: modeling animal coordinated hunting with reinforcement learning. Proc. 43rd CogSci.

The negative control for Imagined We: individually rewarded agents outperform reward-sharing agents, most strongly when hunting is hard and groups large. Read alongside Tang et al. — together a clean example of the model-comparison logic this literature runs on.

Tier 2

Painter, Kim, Renton & Mattingley (2021). Joint control of visually guided actions involves concordant increases in behavioural and neural coupling. Communications Biology, 4(1), 816.

Dual-EEG during joint cursor control, each partner controlling one dimension. The obvious bridge to the neuroscience side — this is what a neural measure of real-time coordination looks like.

Tier 2

Zhao, S. (2020). Coordination games in continuous time. SSRN working paper.

The direct empirical case for continuous time: subjects coordinate significantly better, and reach the payoff-dominant equilibrium more often, than in discrete time — driven by faster within-round learning. Design evidence, to read before committing to a design.

Tier 2

Nalepka, Lamb, Kallen, Shockley, Chemero, Saltzman & Richardson (2019). Human social motor solutions for human–machine interaction in dynamical task contexts. PNAS, 116(4), 1437–1446.

Dynamical-motor-primitive agents coordinate as well as humans and pass a Turing-like test. Relevant twice over: as a partner model for cross-play simulation, and as a bot fallback for unmatched participants.

Tier 2

Nalepka, Kallen, Chemero, Saltzman & Richardson (2017). Herd those sheep: emergent multiagent coordination and behavioral-mode switching. Psychological Science, 28(5), 630–650.

Continuous spatial shepherding. All pairs start with divided-labor “search-and-recover”; a subset spontaneously discover a synchronized oscillatory mode. An entirely different theoretical vocabulary — dynamical systems, attractors, relative phase, HKB — describing the same phenomenon the Bayesian models describe. Read it precisely because it doesn't share their assumptions.

Tier 2

Skyrms (2004). The Stag Hunt and the Evolution of Social Structure. Cambridge University Press.

The modern theoretical treatment: risk-dominant (hare) vs. payoff-dominant (stag), and how network structure and signaling shift which one obtains. Where the paradigm is a stag hunt, this is the framing document. Chapters, not cover-to-cover.

Tier 2

Schmidt, Shupp, Walker & Ostrom (2003). Playing safe in coordination games: the roles of risk dominance, payoff dominance, and history of play. GEB, 42(2), 281–299.

History of play strongly predicts future coordination, and fixed pairing beats random rematching. A direct design constraint: whether partners are stable across trials is a first-order manipulation, not a logistical detail.

Tier 2

Battalio, Samuelson & Van Huyck (2001). Optimization incentives and coordination failure in laboratory stag hunt games. Econometrica, 69(3), 749–764.

The “optimization premium” result: risk dominance alone doesn't predict behavior. Payoff details easily mistaken for arbitrary drive equilibrium selection — meaning the payoff matrix is a substantive theoretical commitment, not a parameter.

Tier 2

Rankin, Van Huyck & Battalio (2000). Strategic similarity and emergent conventions: evidence from similar stag hunt games. GEB, 32(2), 315–337.

Conventions emerge in half of cohorts even with payoffs and labels scrambled every round — and when they do, they track efficiency rather than security or risk dominance. Evidence that people abstract strategic structure across surface variation, i.e. the “abstract goals” claim arriving from the economics side.

Tier 2

Gao, Newman & Scholl (2009) on the psychophysics of chasing · Keller, Novembre & Hove (2014) on rhythm in joint action · Konvalinka et al. (2010) on continuous mutual adaptation in joint tapping · Boesch & Boesch (1989) and Samuni et al. (2018) on chimpanzee cooperative hunting.

Background depth. The Gao one earns its place: people detect chasing rapidly and automatically from motion alone, so in a continuous spatial task partners are reading intentions off trajectories perceptually, below anything that would count as inference. Worth knowing before attributing all of it to a model.

Tier 3
H · collective intelligence & social learning — Wu, Vélez7 items

Adjacent rather than central, and worth being precise about why. This work models social learning — extracting information from others — rather than joint planning, reasoning about a partner's plan toward a shared goal. The foraging paradigms here are congestion problems: peers are simultaneously information sources and competitors for the same resource, with no payoff-dominant joint equilibrium to coordinate onto. That mutual-expectation problem is the entire content of a stag hunt. What this thread does supply, and supplies better than anything in A–G, is the methodological craft for running and fitting real-time multi-agent tasks.

Wu, Deffner, Kahl, Meder, Ho & Kurvers (2025). Adaptive mechanisms of social and asocial learning in immersive collective foraging. Nature Communications, 16, 3539.

Ho is a co-author, and this is the best methodological template available for building a real-time task. It solves the three hard problems: how to define a discrete decision event inside continuous movement, how to reconstruct what each agent could actually see of the other (automated visual-field transcription — essential for conditioning a model on observability), and how to fit a time-varying-weight choice model to trajectory data. Public data on Zenodo. Read the methods, not just the results.

03

on “simulation experiments”

Papers in this list are frequently described as running “simulation experiments” to compare models of coordination. The phrase covers at least five distinct activities, and conflating them is a common source of confusion.

1 · Forward simulation — do the models even differ?

Implement three or four competing accounts — level-k / cognitive hierarchy, joint-intention planning, virtual bargaining, plain best-response — run them as agents in a candidate environment, and look at what comes out. The first question is basic and constantly skipped: do these models produce different behavior at all? In many environments, level-2 reasoning and team reasoning prescribe the same action, and the equations alone will not reveal it.

2 · Model recovery

Simulate a dataset from Model A, then fit every model to that synthetic dataset and see which wins. Repeat with B as generator, then C, then D. The result is a confusion matrix: generating model × recovered model. If A's data is best fit by B, the models are not identifiable in that task, and no quantity of human data will fix it. The same exercise at the parameter level asks whether known parameters can be recovered from the number of trials a single participant realistically produces.

3 · Design optimization

Once models can be simulated and recovered, the environment becomes something to search over. Sweep layouts, payoff structures, visibility conditions, time pressure; score each candidate by how far apart the models' predictions fall, or by expected information gain about which model is true. This is optimal experimental design, and it is why simulation properly precedes task construction rather than following it.

4 · Cross-play

The genuinely multi-agent part. In single-agent modeling, simulating a model means running one agent; here behavior is a joint product of two policies, which changes the object being evaluated. What is needed is an N×N pairing matrix — A with A, A with B, B with A. Self-play is famously misleading: the Overcooked result is precisely that an agent optimized in self-play can be near-useless with a partner it was not trained against. Human data is always cross-play, since people are never paired with copies of themselves. A related decision silently breaks model fits if left implicit: when fitting one participant's choices, what is assumed about their partner? Fitting each person as though their partner were optimal will systematically mis-attribute coordination failures to the wrong parameter.

5 · Power analysis

Simulate N participants × T trials, run the planned model-comparison analysis on the synthetic data, and count how often it selects the true model. That curve over N is a sample-size justification that can be preregistered. It matters more here than in a typical study because synchronous two-player recruitment is lossy — a meaningful fraction of recruited participants produce no usable data.

A second sense of the phrase

“Simulation experiments” sometimes instead means agent-based or evolutionary simulation, where the question is not which model fits humans but which coordination mechanism is stable, learnable, or evolvable. Kleiman-Weiner et al. (2025, PNAS) is the current exemplar. That is a legitimate research program in its own right and requires no human data at all.
